How it works

A walkthrough of what was built, why, and what was deliberately left out. No engineering background needed.

This is a corporate card spend service. A company issues cards to employees, each with a spend limit. When an employee pays for something, the merchant asks us "can this card cover $X right now?" — and we answer approved or declined, in one call, and permanently record what we said.

Two things make that harder than it sounds, and they are what the build is really about: the same charge must never post twice, and two charges arriving at the same instant must never both slip past the same limit.

The one rule that must never break: approved spend on a card can never exceed that card's limit — no matter how many charges arrive at once, or how many times a nervous client retries.

Try it in 60 seconds

The app boots with three cards and six charges already seeded, one of which was declined.

  1. Open the Cards tab — three cards, each showing its limit, spend so far, and what's left.
  2. Note Ada Lovelace: a $2,500.00 limit with $101.99 spent. A $2,500.00 charge to Dell was declined — $2,500.00 no longer fits under what's left.
  3. Switch to the Transactions tab. The declined Dell charge is still there, with the reason LIMIT_EXCEEDED. Declines are records, not errors.
  4. Freeze a card from the Cards tab, then try to charge it. Declined, reason CARD_FROZEN — same shape of answer, different reason.

What happens when a charge arrives

Four decisions worth knowing about

A decline is a successful answer, not an error

"This card can't cover it" is the system working correctly. So a decline comes back as a normal, successful response carrying the outcome, and it is saved. The decline history is a product surface in its own right — it answers disputes, feeds fraud review, and is the first thing a cardholder asks about. Throwing it away as an error would delete the most-asked-about data in the system.

Retrying a charge can't double-charge

If the network drops after we approve but before the merchant hears back, they will retry. Every charge carries a client-generated key; the database physically refuses to store two charges with the same key. A retry gets the original answer back, and the money moves once. This is the same mechanism Stripe exposes as Idempotency-Key.

Two simultaneous charges can't both squeak under the limit

A card with $50 left, hit by two $50 charges at the same millisecond, is the classic way a limit leaks. Step 2 above prevents it: the first request locks the card, decides, and writes; the second waits, then reads a total that already includes the first. Exactly one is approved. There is a test that fires eight concurrent charges and asserts precisely one wins — sequential tests pass happily against broken code here, so that test is the proof.

The balance is calculated, never stored

A card's remaining balance is added up from its approved charges on every read, rather than kept as a running number that gets updated. A stored counter can drift out of sync with reality after a crash or a bug, and reconciling it is painful. Deriving it means the number shown is, by construction, the truth. At high volume you'd cache a running total and reconcile it against this calculation nightly — that's a scaling change, not a redesign.

How the pieces fit together

The data

Card

Issued to a person, carries a limit, never deleted.

  • cardholder name and last four digits only — the full number is deliberately never stored or logged
  • status: ACTIVE FROZEN CANCELLED
  • spend limit + currency

Active and frozen convert back and forth; either can be cancelled; cancelled is permanent. There is no delete endpoint — financial records are cancelled, not erased.

Transaction

One authorization attempt. Immutable once written.

  • amount + currency, merchant, timestamp
  • outcome: APPROVED DECLINED
  • decline reason, when declined
  • the client's idempotency key

Amounts are whole numbers of cents, never decimals. Decimal arithmetic on money loses fractions of a cent, and those errors accumulate into real reconciliation problems.

Deliberately not built

These are scoping decisions, not oversights. Each one is a conversation, not a missing feature — and each has a known shape if it becomes a requirement.

  • Real authentication. The caller states who they are in a header. Everything is wired so that swapping in real sign-in touches exactly one class.
  • Settlement and refunds. This answers the authorization question — "can this go through" — not the later movement of money, nor credits back.
  • Multi-currency conversion. A card is denominated in one currency and rejects charges in another, rather than guessing an exchange rate.
  • Spend controls beyond the limit. Merchant categories, per-day caps, and approval workflows are all natural next features on top of this model.
  • A real database and queues. Everything runs in memory so the whole thing starts with one command. The path to production scale is written up in the architecture notes rather than half-built here.
